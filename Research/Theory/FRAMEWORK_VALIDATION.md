# Purpose

This document critically evaluates the internal coherence, novelty, applicability, and empirical testability of the proposed **Computational Facilitation Framework**.

It is not intended for public release in its current form.

Its purpose is to challenge the framework before formal manuscript preparation begins.

The validation process seeks to determine:

* whether the framework addresses a genuine research problem;
* whether its concepts are internally consistent;
* whether it differs meaningfully from established paradigms;
* whether its methods can be applied reproducibly;
* whether its claims can be supported or challenged through evidence;
* whether the framework improves the design or evaluation of computational systems.

The objective is not to defend Computational Facilitation uncritically.

The objective is to identify weaknesses early enough for the framework to be refined, narrowed, renamed, or rejected where necessary.

---

# Validation Philosophy

A conceptual framework becomes scientifically useful only when it can be:

* defined precisely;
* compared with existing work;
* applied consistently;
* criticized meaningfully;
* examined empirically;
* revised in response to evidence.

The existence of a reference implementation does not, by itself, validate the framework.

Likewise, philosophical appeal does not establish novelty, explanatory value, or practical usefulness.

The framework should therefore be evaluated according to both conceptual and empirical standards.

The internal review is organized around the following primary questions.

1. Does Computational Facilitation address a problem not already adequately addressed by existing frameworks?

2. Are its major concepts defined clearly and consistently?

3. Does Intentional Responsibility Allocation follow logically from the central proposition?

4. Does the proposed evaluation framework measure the intended ideas?

7. What evidence would support, weaken, or falsify its claims?

8. Does the framework improve system design compared with existing practice?

9. In which domains is the framework useful?

10. In which domains should it not be applied?

# Current Framework Structure

The current theoretical structure is:

```text
Computational Facilitation
        ↓
Foundational Assumptions
        ↓
Core Design Principles
        ↓
Intentional Responsibility Allocation
        ↓
System Implementation
        ↓
Computational and Human Evaluation
```

The framework currently contains three principal layers.

## Layer 1 — Computational Facilitation

The overarching human-centered design philosophy.

It proposes that computation should automate objective, deterministic, repetitive, and infrastructural responsibilities while preserving human responsibility for interpretation, creativity, negotiation, judgment, and meaning when those activities constitute the primary purpose of the system.

## Layer 2 — Intentional Responsibility Allocation

The proposed design methodology.

It requires designers to identify, classify, and explicitly allocate system responsibilities between humans and computational components.

## Layer 3 — Computational Facilitation Evaluation Framework

The proposed evaluation methodology.

It examines:

* purpose alignment;
* responsibility allocation;
* human outcomes;
* computational outcomes;
* overall balance.

This validation document evaluates whether these three layers are sufficiently distinct, logically connected, and practically useful.

# Internal Consistency Review

## Question 1 — Does the central proposition follow from the stated assumptions?

The central proposition is:

> Computational systems need not maximize automation in order to maximize value.

This proposition is supported by the following assumptions:

* some activities derive value directly from human participation;
* automation may alter the purpose of an activity;
* humans and computational systems possess complementary strengths;
* responsibility allocation is a design choice;
* infrastructure should be distinguished from the purpose of an activity.

The proposition appears consistent with these assumptions.

However, the framework must avoid implying that human participation is inherently valuable in every context.

A more defensible formulation is:

> Where meaningful human participation constitutes part of an activity's intended value, automation should be evaluated according to whether it strengthens or displaces that participation.

This conditional formulation better preserves the framework's stated boundary conditions.

---

## Question 2 — Does Intentional Responsibility Allocation follow logically from Computational Facilitation?

Computational Facilitation proposes that responsibilities should be distributed according to the purpose of the activity.

Intentional Responsibility Allocation operationalizes this by requiring designers to:

1. define the activity's purpose;
2. identify responsibilities;
3. classify those responsibilities;
4. allocate them explicitly;
5. evaluate the resulting human and computational outcomes.

The methodology therefore follows logically from the philosophy.

However, the current name may understate the role of purpose.

The allocation is not merely intentional.

It is purpose-guided.

Possible alternative names include:

* Purpose-Guided Responsibility Allocation;
* Purpose-Driven Responsibility Allocation;
* Activity-Aligned Responsibility Allocation;
* Human-Purpose Responsibility Allocation.

No terminology change should be adopted until the literature review establishes whether similar terms already exist.

---

## Question 3 — Does the evaluation framework assess the design methodology?

The evaluation framework examines whether:

* automation supports the activity's purpose;
* responsibilities are explicitly allocated;
* human outcomes are preserved or strengthened;
* computational responsibilities are performed reliably;
* the overall arrangement remains balanced.

These dimensions correspond reasonably well to the design methodology.

A potential weakness is that the term **balance** may be interpreted as an equal distribution of responsibility.

The framework does not require equality.

It requires appropriateness.

The evaluation dimension may therefore be better named:

> **Allocation Fitness**

or:

> **Purpose–Allocation Alignment**

This would clarify that a system may appropriately assign most responsibilities to computation when extensive automation supports the system's purpose.

# Conceptual Dependency Review

The framework currently depends upon the following causal sequence:

```text
Purpose
        ↓
Responsibility Identification
        ↓
Responsibility Allocation
        ↓
System Behaviour
        ↓
Human and Computational Outcomes
```

This sequence is conceptually plausible but not yet empirically established.

Several assumptions require validation.

## Assumption A

Clearer definition of system purpose leads to more defensible responsibility allocation.

## Assumption B

Responsibility allocation affects human participation.

## Assumption C

Human participation affects outcomes such as engagement, learning, creativity, or collaboration.

## Assumption D

Purpose-aligned allocation produces better outcomes than allocation driven primarily by available technology.

These assumptions provide a possible basis for empirical studies.

# Preliminary Novelty Assessment

The framework should not claim that human-centered system design, division of labour, responsibility allocation, or the preservation of human agency are entirely new ideas.

Related concepts are likely present within:

* Human-Centered Computing;
* Human-Computer Interaction;
* Computer-Supported Cooperative Work;
* Activity Theory;
* Distributed Cognition;
* Mixed-Initiative Interaction;
* Human-in-the-Loop Systems;
* Value Sensitive Design;
* Participatory Design;
* Responsible AI;
* Automation allocation research;
* Joint cognitive systems;
* function-allocation literature.

The potential contribution of Computational Facilitation may instead lie in the combination of four elements:

1. treating the **purpose of the human activity** as the primary allocation criterion;

2. distinguishing **infrastructural responsibilities** from responsibilities that constitute the activity's substantive purpose;

3. preserving Human ↔ Human interaction as an explicit design outcome;

4. connecting responsibility allocation to a combined evaluation of computational performance and human outcomes.

This potential contribution remains provisional until a systematic literature review is completed.

---

# Novelty Risks

## Risk 1 — Reinvention of Function Allocation

Human-factors and automation research has long examined how functions should be allocated between humans and machines.

Computational Facilitation may overlap substantially with this literature.

Required action:

* review function-allocation theory;
* identify whether purpose-guided allocation has already been formalized;
* determine whether the framework provides a distinct application or synthesis.

---

## Risk 2 — Reinvention of Human-Centered Computing

The framework may be interpreted as a restatement of established human-centered design principles.

Required action:

* identify the exact problem that existing HCC approaches leave insufficiently specified;
* clarify why responsibility allocation deserves independent treatment.

---

## Risk 3 — Overlap with Value Sensitive Design

Value Sensitive Design explicitly integrates human values into technology design.

Computational Facilitation may overlap through its concern for agency, responsibility, and meaningful interaction.

Required action:

* distinguish responsibility allocation from value identification;
* examine whether Computational Facilitation could be positioned as an operational method compatible with Value Sensitive Design.

---

## Risk 4 — Overlap with Activity Theory and Distributed Cognition

Both traditions analyse how activities and cognition are distributed across people, tools, and environments.

Required action:

* determine whether Computational Facilitation adds a normative allocation method rather than only a descriptive analysis;
* avoid claiming novelty where the framework instead synthesizes established insights.

---

# Provisional Novelty Statement

Until the literature review is complete, the strongest defensible novelty claim is:

> Computational Facilitation is a proposed responsibility-allocation framework for designing systems in which meaningful Human ↔ Human interaction forms part of the intended value. It organizes system design around the purpose of the activity, distinguishes infrastructural from meaning-bearing responsibilities, and evaluates both computational performance and human outcomes.

This statement should remain provisional.

The final manuscript must revise it after systematic comparison with existing frameworks.

# Terminology Review

## Computational Facilitation

Strengths:

* concise;
* broadly applicable;
* emphasizes support rather than control;
* not limited to artificial intelligence.

Risks:

* the term may already exist in other disciplines;
* “facilitation” may appear vague;
* the term alone does not communicate responsibility allocation.

Required action:

* conduct exact-term and concept searches;
* identify prior uses;
* determine whether a more specific name is necessary.

---

## Intentional Responsibility Allocation

Strengths:

* explicitly names the allocation mechanism;
* applies to humans and computational systems;
* avoids reducing the question to automation quantity.

Risks:

* “intentional” may be too weak;
* responsibility may be difficult to distinguish from function, task, decision, role, or authority;
* the abbreviation IRA may already be heavily used in unrelated fields.

Required action:

* compare responsibility with alternative units of analysis;
* evaluate alternative terminology;
* avoid adopting an acronym before the literature review.

---

## Computational Facilitation Evaluation Framework

Strengths:

* directly connects evaluation to the framework;
* distinguishes design from assessment.

Risks:

* the name is long;
* the proposed dimensions are not yet validated;
* a named evaluation framework may imply greater maturity than currently exists.

Required action:

* retain as a working term only;
* do not present it as a standardized instrument until reliability and validity are assessed.

# Unit of Analysis

The current unit of analysis is the **responsibility**.

This choice requires further scrutiny.

Possible alternative units include:

* function;
* task;
* decision;
* authority;
* interaction;
* cognitive operation;
* social role.

Responsibility may be advantageous because it includes both the performance of an activity and accountability for its outcome.

However, it may also be too abstract for consistent coding.

A future validation exercise should compare whether independent evaluators can reliably identify responsibilities within the same system.

The framework should retain responsibility as its provisional unit of analysis until comparative testing determines whether another unit offers greater clarity or reproducibility.

# Reproducibility Assessment

A useful framework should be applicable by researchers and designers other than its original developers.

Computational Facilitation therefore requires evaluation of inter-rater consistency.

The central question is:

> Can independent evaluators examine the same system and reach reasonably comparable conclusions regarding its purpose, responsibilities, allocations, and outcomes?

Perfect agreement is not expected.

However, persistent disagreement would indicate that the framework requires clearer definitions, coding rules, or domain-specific guidance.

---

# Proposed Reproducibility Exercise

A preliminary reproducibility study should provide multiple evaluators with the same set of computational systems.

Possible systems include:

* The Last Word War™;
* a collaborative document editor;
* a video-conferencing platform;
* an AI writing assistant;
* a learning-management system;
* a scientific peer-review platform.

Each evaluator should independently complete the following tasks.

1. Define the system's primary purpose.

2. Identify its major responsibilities.

3. Classify each responsibility.

4. Record whether the responsibility is assigned primarily to humans, computation, or both.

5. Evaluate purpose alignment.

6. Evaluate likely human outcomes.

7. Evaluate computational outcomes.

8. Provide an overall assessment of allocation fitness.

Results should then be compared across evaluators.

---

# Reproducibility Questions

The exercise should investigate:

* Do evaluators define the same primary purpose?
* Do they identify similar responsibilities?
* Do they classify responsibilities consistently?
* Do they agree on who holds each responsibility?
* Do they reach comparable conclusions about purpose alignment?
* Which concepts generate the greatest disagreement?

High disagreement would indicate a need to revise definitions or provide clearer application guidance.

# Empirical Testability

The framework should generate claims that can be investigated empirically.

It should not remain limited to retrospective description.

Several provisional hypotheses follow from the current theory.

---

## Hypothesis 1 — Purpose-Aligned Allocation

Systems whose responsibility allocations are explicitly aligned with the activity's primary purpose will produce more favourable human outcomes than otherwise comparable systems whose allocations are driven mainly by technical capability.

Possible outcomes include:

* engagement;
* collaboration;
* learning;
* creativity;
* participant satisfaction.

---

## Hypothesis 2 — Preservation of Meaning-Bearing Responsibilities

Where interpretation, negotiation, or creativity constitutes part of an activity's intended value, preserving these responsibilities for humans will increase meaningful participation compared with systems that automate them.

---

## Hypothesis 3 — Infrastructural Automation

Automating repetitive, objective, and infrastructural responsibilities will improve reliability and reduce unnecessary participant burden without reducing meaningful human participation.

---

## Hypothesis 4 — Excessive Computational Substitution

When computation assumes responsibilities that constitute the substantive purpose of an activity, participants will demonstrate lower ownership, weaker interaction, or reduced engagement.

---

## Hypothesis 5 — Allocation Transparency

Systems that make responsibility allocation clear to participants will produce greater understanding of human and computational roles than systems whose allocations remain implicit.

These hypotheses remain provisional and require refinement before formal testing.

---

# Possible Empirical Designs

Several study designs may be used to evaluate the framework.

---

## Comparative System Study

Compare two systems supporting the same activity but allocating responsibilities differently.

Example:

* one language-learning system preserves learner-to-learner conversation;
* another replaces conversation with AI-generated dialogue.

Measure:

* engagement;
* learning outcomes;
* perceived ownership;
* interaction quality;
* willingness to continue.

---

## Controlled Feature Study

Create two versions of the same system.

Version A preserves a selected human responsibility.

Version B automates that responsibility.

All other system characteristics should remain as similar as possible.

This design would help isolate the effect of responsibility allocation.

---

## Longitudinal Study

Observe how responsibility allocation influences behaviour over repeated use.

Potential outcomes include:

* skill development;
* dependence on automation;
* changes in participation;
* changes in collaborative behaviour.

---

## Designer Study

Ask independent design teams to apply the responsibility-allocation methodology to the same design problem.

Compare:

* identified responsibilities;
* allocation decisions;
* design rationales;
* resulting prototypes.

This would test the practical reproducibility of the methodology.

---

## Retrospective Case Analysis

Apply the framework to existing systems that were not designed using Computational Facilitation.

This may reveal whether the framework provides useful explanatory or evaluative insight beyond its reference implementation.

---

# Falsification and Revision Conditions

The framework should be revised if empirical evidence repeatedly demonstrates any of the following.

## Condition 1

Purpose-aligned responsibility allocation does not improve relevant human outcomes.

## Condition 2

Systems that automate meaning-bearing responsibilities consistently produce equal or better outcomes without reducing meaningful participation.

## Condition 3

Independent evaluators cannot identify or classify responsibilities with acceptable consistency.

## Condition 4

The framework produces no design recommendations beyond those already generated by established human-centered methods.

## Condition 5

Responsibility allocation proves less useful than alternative units such as tasks, functions, roles, or decisions.

## Condition 6

The proposed evaluation dimensions cannot be operationalized reliably.

Such findings may narrow the framework's scope, modify its terminology, or challenge its central assumptions.

---

# Alternative Explanations

Observed positive outcomes in systems associated with Computational Facilitation may arise from factors other than responsibility allocation.

Possible alternative explanations include:

* novelty effects;
* interface quality;
* participant familiarity;
* social relationships among participants;
* game mechanics unrelated to the framework;
* observer influence;
* small sample sizes;
* selection bias.

Future empirical studies should attempt to isolate responsibility allocation from these competing explanations.

---

# Reference Implementation Risk

The Last Word War™ is both the origin of the framework and its first implementation.

This creates a risk of circular reasoning.

For example:

1. the framework is derived from the design of The Last Word War™;
2. The Last Word War™ is then presented as evidence supporting the framework.

The reference implementation can demonstrate feasibility, but it cannot independently establish general validity.

Broader validation therefore requires:

* external application domains;
* independent designers;
* comparative systems;
* empirical studies not limited to the game.

---

# Current Evidence Assessment

The current evidence base includes:

* documented design evolution;
* explicit responsibility allocation;
* an operational multiplayer implementation;
* early playtesting observations;
* player feedback mechanisms;
* engineering validation records.

This evidence supports the feasibility of applying the framework within one project.

It does not yet establish:

* generalizability;
* causal effects;
* reproducibility;
* superiority over alternative frameworks;
* validated evaluation instruments.

The current confidence level should therefore be described as:

> **Conceptually promising, operationally demonstrated in one case, and empirically preliminary.**

# Closing Assessment

Computational Facilitation currently represents a coherent and potentially useful conceptual framework.

Its strongest features are:

* purpose-guided system analysis;
* explicit responsibility allocation;
* distinction between infrastructure and meaning-bearing activity;
* combined evaluation of computational and human outcomes.

Its principal weaknesses are:

* uncertain novelty;
* limited empirical evidence;
* possible overlap with established function-allocation and HCI traditions;
* unresolved terminology;
* untested reproducibility.

The framework should therefore remain classified as an active theoretical proposal.

Its transition into a formal scholarly contribution depends upon rigorous literature mapping, independent application, empirical testing, and willingness to revise or narrow its claims.
