# Purpose

This document develops the theoretical foundation of **Computational Facilitation**, a proposed human-centered design framework for computational systems.

Unlike a research manuscript, this document is intended to serve as a working theory notebook.

Its purpose is to refine definitions, assumptions, principles, limitations, examples, criticisms, and research questions before formal publication.

Ideas contained within this document should be regarded as evolving and subject to revision as new evidence becomes available.

---

# Motivation

Modern computational systems increasingly participate in activities that were once performed exclusively by humans.

Advances in artificial intelligence have enabled computers to generate text, images, software, music, scientific hypotheses, and other forms of content that previously required substantial human effort.

While these capabilities create new opportunities, they also raise an important design question:

> **What responsibilities should computational systems assume, and which responsibilities should remain intentionally human?**

Many existing approaches seek to optimize computational capability.

Computational Facilitation instead asks a different question:

> **How should computational systems be designed when the primary objective is to strengthen Human ↔ Human interaction rather than replace it?**

This document explores that question.

---

# Working Definition

## Computational Facilitation

**Computational Facilitation is a human-centered design framework in which computational systems intentionally automate objective, deterministic, and infrastructural responsibilities while deliberately preserving human responsibility for interpretation, creativity, negotiation, judgment, and meaning whenever those activities constitute the primary purpose of the system.**

Within this framework, computation functions primarily as an enabler of human interaction rather than as its replacement.

---

# Why "Facilitation"?

The term *facilitation* is chosen deliberately.

A facilitator does not normally determine outcomes.

Instead, a facilitator:

- organizes;
- coordinates;
- synchronizes;
- records;
- supports;
- enables.

The participants themselves remain responsible for the substance of the activity.

Computational Facilitation proposes that many digital systems should perform a similar role.

Rather than replacing human participation, computational systems should create conditions under which meaningful human interaction can occur more effectively.

---

# Scope

Computational Facilitation is intended as a general design framework rather than a framework specific to artificial intelligence.

The framework applies to any computational system—including AI-based systems—whose primary purpose is to support human activities involving communication, collaboration, creativity, education, or decision-making.

Potential application domains include:

- multiplayer games;
- educational platforms;
- collaborative writing;
- peer review systems;
- scientific collaboration;
- brainstorming environments;
- deliberation platforms;
- knowledge-sharing communities.

The Last Word War™ serves as the initial case study rather than the sole intended application.

---

# Relationship to Human-Centered Computing

Human-Centered Computing is an established interdisciplinary field that seeks to design computational systems around human needs, capabilities, and experiences.

Computational Facilitation does not seek to replace Human-Centered Computing.

Instead, it proposes a more specific design framework addressing an increasingly important question:

> **How should computational responsibilities be allocated in systems whose primary purpose is meaningful Human ↔ Human interaction?**

In this sense, Computational Facilitation may be viewed as one possible design framework operating within the broader discipline of Human-Centered Computing.

---

# Central Proposition

The central proposition of Computational Facilitation is:

> **Computational systems need not maximize automation in order to maximize value.**

Instead, value may often be increased by deliberately preserving those human responsibilities that constitute the intellectual, creative, interpretive, or social purpose of an activity, while allowing computation to manage the objective infrastructure that enables those activities.

This proposition forms the theoretical foundation for the remainder of this framework.

---

# Foundational Assumptions

Computational Facilitation is founded upon several assumptions regarding the relationship between humans and computational systems.

These assumptions define the scope of the framework and establish the conditions under which it is intended to apply.

The assumptions are not presented as universal truths. Rather, they represent the philosophical foundations upon which the framework is constructed.

---

## Assumption 1 — Humans Are the Primary Bearers of Meaning

Meaning does not arise solely from computational processes.

Although computational systems may manipulate symbols, patterns, and representations, the interpretation of those representations ultimately depends upon human participants.

Computational systems may assist the creation, organization, or transmission of information, but meaning itself remains grounded in human interpretation.

Consequently, systems whose primary purpose is the creation or exchange of meaning should preserve meaningful opportunities for human interpretation.

---

## Assumption 2 — Not Every Task Benefits from Maximum Automation

Automation is not an objective in itself.

Whether automation is desirable depends upon the purpose of the activity being supported.

For tasks whose value lies primarily in efficiency, consistency, or precision, greater automation may be appropriate.

However, for tasks whose value derives from creativity, discussion, interpretation, learning, or social interaction, excessive automation may reduce the very qualities that make the activity worthwhile.

Computational Facilitation therefore evaluates automation according to its effect on the purpose of the activity rather than according to the amount of automation achieved.

---

## Assumption 3 — Computational Systems and Humans Possess Complementary Strengths

Humans and computational systems exhibit different capabilities.

Computational systems generally excel at:

- deterministic execution;
- synchronization;
- storage;
- repetition;
- large-scale computation;
- rule enforcement.

Humans generally excel at:

- interpretation;
- contextual reasoning;
- creativity;
- ethical judgment;
- negotiation;
- empathy;
- assigning meaning.

Computational Facilitation assumes that systems should be designed to exploit these complementary strengths rather than attempting to eliminate one in favour of the other.

---

## Assumption 4 — Human Agency Is a Design Choice

Human agency is not automatically preserved within computational systems.

Every computational system implicitly allocates responsibilities between humans and technology.

These allocations arise from design decisions.

Consequently, preserving meaningful human participation should be understood as an intentional design objective rather than an inevitable consequence of using computational technology.

---

## Assumption 5 — Infrastructure and Purpose Should Be Distinguished

Many computational systems simultaneously perform two fundamentally different functions.

The first function concerns infrastructure.

Examples include:

- communication;
- synchronization;
- storage;
- timing;
- persistence;
- authentication.

The second function concerns the purpose of the activity itself.

Examples include:

- discussion;
- creativity;
- education;
- collaboration;
- deliberation;
- artistic expression.

Computational Facilitation proposes that computational systems should primarily automate infrastructure while preserving human responsibility for activities constituting the purpose of the interaction.

---

# Core Principles

The framework proposes several design principles derived from these foundational assumptions.

These principles provide guidance for the design and evaluation of computational systems.

---

## Principle 1 — Preserve Human Responsibility

Whenever the primary value of an activity depends upon human judgment, interpretation, creativity, or negotiation, these responsibilities should remain primarily human.

Computational systems should avoid unnecessarily replacing these functions.

---

## Principle 2 — Automate Objective Infrastructure

Whenever responsibilities are objective, deterministic, repetitive, or infrastructural, computational systems should perform these responsibilities whenever doing so improves reliability, efficiency, or accessibility.

Examples include:

- timing;
- synchronization;
- persistent storage;
- version control;
- deterministic rule enforcement.

---

## Principle 3 — Facilitate Rather Than Direct

Whenever possible, computational systems should enable productive interaction without determining its substantive outcome.

A facilitator organizes participation.

A director determines participation.

Computational Facilitation intentionally favours the former.

---

## Principle 4 — Preserve Interpretive Freedom

Participants should retain meaningful opportunities to interpret, negotiate, and construct shared understanding.

The computational system should provide structure without prescribing meaning.

---

## Principle 5 — Evaluate Systems by Human Outcomes

The effectiveness of a computational system should not be evaluated solely through computational performance.

Evaluation should additionally consider human outcomes, including:

- quality of interaction;
- engagement;
- learning;
- collaboration;
- creativity;
- participant satisfaction;
- preservation of agency.

These outcomes constitute an essential component of system performance under Computational Facilitation.

---

# Relationship to Existing Paradigms

Computational Facilitation is not intended to replace existing theories of human-centered system design.

Rather, it seeks to address a specific question that has become increasingly important as computational systems assume greater responsibility within human activities:

> **How should responsibilities be allocated between computational systems and humans when the primary purpose of a system is meaningful Human ↔ Human interaction?**

Several established research areas provide important foundations for this work.

Computational Facilitation builds upon these traditions while proposing a more explicit framework for responsibility allocation.

---

# Human-Centered Computing

Human-Centered Computing (HCC) is a broad interdisciplinary field concerned with designing computational systems around human needs, capabilities, and experiences.

Within HCC, emphasis is typically placed on usability, accessibility, user experience, and human values.

Computational Facilitation is consistent with these objectives.

However, it narrows the focus to a specific design problem:

> **Which responsibilities should remain human, and which should be delegated to computational systems?**

Rather than concentrating primarily on user experience, Computational Facilitation emphasizes intentional responsibility allocation.

---

# Human-in-the-Loop Systems

Human-in-the-loop systems deliberately include humans within computational decision-making processes.

These systems recognize that certain decisions require human oversight or intervention.

Computational Facilitation shares this recognition but differs in emphasis.

Human-in-the-loop approaches often begin with computational processes and identify points where human input improves system performance.

Computational Facilitation begins with human activities and asks which computational responsibilities should exist in the first place.

This difference shifts the design perspective from optimization to intentional allocation.

---

# Mixed-Initiative Interaction

Mixed-initiative systems allow humans and computational systems to contribute jointly toward solving shared tasks.

Initiative may shift dynamically between participants.

Computational Facilitation is compatible with mixed-initiative interaction.

However, it introduces an additional design consideration.

Rather than asking:

> Who currently has initiative?

it asks:

> Who ought to retain responsibility for different aspects of the activity?

Responsibility and initiative therefore become related but distinct concepts.

---

# AI-Assisted Creativity

AI-assisted creative systems use computational models to support activities such as writing, programming, music composition, and visual art.

These systems frequently generate candidate ideas or complete portions of creative work.

Computational Facilitation does not reject AI-assisted creativity.

Instead, it proposes that designers should explicitly evaluate whether computational participation enhances or diminishes the human purpose of the creative activity.

The framework therefore encourages intentional boundaries rather than maximum automation.

---

# Participatory Design

Participatory Design emphasizes involving stakeholders throughout the design process.

Computational Facilitation complements this tradition.

Participatory Design focuses primarily on how systems are designed.

Computational Facilitation focuses primarily on how completed systems distribute ongoing responsibilities during use.

The two perspectives therefore address different stages of a system's lifecycle.

---

# Responsible AI

Responsible AI seeks to ensure that computational systems operate ethically, transparently, and fairly.

Important concerns include:

- accountability;
- transparency;
- privacy;
- fairness;
- safety.

Computational Facilitation shares many of these concerns.

Its primary contribution lies elsewhere.

Rather than concentrating exclusively on ethical properties of computational systems, it asks whether certain human responsibilities should remain intentionally non-automated regardless of technical capability.

This introduces responsibility allocation as an additional design dimension alongside traditional Responsible AI principles.

---

# Distinguishing Characteristics

Computational Facilitation differs from existing paradigms through several distinguishing characteristics.

| Dimension | Computational Facilitation |
|-----------|----------------------------|
| Primary focus | Responsibility allocation |
| Core objective | Preserve meaningful Human ↔ Human interaction |
| Role of computation | Facilitate rather than replace |
| Human role | Interpretation, creativity, negotiation, judgment, meaning |
| Computational role | Infrastructure, coordination, synchronization, deterministic processing |
| Evaluation | Human outcomes as well as computational performance |

These characteristics should be viewed as complementary to existing research rather than competitive with it.

---

# Position Within the Research Landscape

Computational Facilitation may therefore be understood as a specialized design framework situated within Human-Centered Computing.

It incorporates insights from:

- Human-Computer Interaction;
- Human-Centered Computing;
- Human-in-the-Loop Systems;
- Mixed-Initiative Interaction;
- Responsible AI;
- Participatory Design.

Its distinctive contribution is the explicit allocation of responsibilities between computational systems and humans according to the purpose of the activity being supported.

Rather than maximizing automation, the framework proposes that successful system design sometimes requires deliberately preserving opportunities for human interpretation, creativity, collaboration, and judgment.

---

# Intentional Responsibility Allocation

The central design mechanism of Computational Facilitation is **Intentional Responsibility Allocation (IRA)**.

Rather than asking how much of a system should be automated, IRA asks a different question:

> **Which responsibilities should be assigned to computational systems, and which should remain intentionally human?**

Computational Facilitation argues that this allocation should be guided primarily by the purpose of the activity rather than by technological capability.

The objective is not to minimize automation nor to maximize human involvement.

Instead, the objective is to assign responsibilities to the participant—human or computational—that is best suited to fulfil them while preserving the essential purpose of the activity.

---

# Responsibility Domains

Computational Facilitation distinguishes between two broad categories of responsibility.

## Computational Responsibilities

Computational systems are generally well suited to responsibilities that are:

- objective;
- deterministic;
- repetitive;
- infrastructural;
- time-sensitive;
- computationally intensive.

Examples include:

- synchronization;
- timing;
- persistent storage;
- authentication;
- deterministic rule enforcement;
- state management;
- network communication;
- data integrity.

These responsibilities benefit from automation because consistency and precision are desirable.

---

## Human Responsibilities

Humans are generally better suited to responsibilities involving:

- interpretation;
- creativity;
- contextual reasoning;
- ethical judgment;
- negotiation;
- empathy;
- assigning meaning;
- social interaction.

These responsibilities derive their value from human participation itself.

Automating them may fundamentally alter the purpose of the activity.

---

# Design Procedure

Computational Facilitation proposes the following design process.

## Step 1 — Define the Primary Purpose

Identify the activity that the system exists to support.

Examples include:

- conversation;
- learning;
- collaboration;
- creativity;
- decision-making.

The purpose should be expressed independently of any proposed technology.

---

## Step 2 — Identify Responsibilities

List every significant responsibility required for the activity.

Both computational and human responsibilities should be included.

---

## Step 3 — Classify Responsibilities

Determine whether each responsibility is primarily:

- infrastructural;
- procedural;
- interpretive;
- creative;
- social;
- ethical.

Some responsibilities may span multiple categories.

---

## Step 4 — Allocate Responsibilities

Assign each responsibility to the participant most appropriate for performing it.

The allocation should preserve the purpose of the activity while allowing computation to improve efficiency where appropriate.

---

## Step 5 — Evaluate the Allocation

The proposed allocation should be reviewed using the following questions.

- Does automation improve the activity or merely replace it?
- Does the allocation preserve meaningful human participation?
- Has computation assumed responsibilities requiring human judgment?
- Are humans unnecessarily performing deterministic tasks?
- Does the resulting system strengthen the intended activity?

---

# Evaluation Criteria

A system designed using Computational Facilitation should be evaluated across two complementary dimensions.

## Computational Performance

Examples include:

- correctness;
- reliability;
- synchronization;
- scalability;
- latency;
- fault tolerance.

---

## Human Outcomes

Examples include:

- engagement;
- collaboration;
- creativity;
- learning;
- communication quality;
- participant satisfaction;
- preservation of agency.

Neither dimension alone is sufficient.

A technically excellent system that diminishes meaningful human participation may fail to satisfy the objectives of Computational Facilitation.

Similarly, a highly engaging system that performs unreliably may fail to provide adequate computational support.

The framework therefore encourages balanced evaluation across both computational and human dimensions.

---

# Boundary Conditions

Computational Facilitation is not intended to discourage automation universally.

Many systems legitimately require extensive automation.

Examples include:

- industrial process control;
- numerical simulation;
- autonomous navigation;
- network routing;
- medical image reconstruction.

In such domains, the primary objective is often accuracy, speed, consistency, or safety.

The framework is instead intended for systems whose principal value depends upon meaningful human participation.

Examples include:

- educational environments;
- collaborative creativity;
- multiplayer interaction;
- discussion platforms;
- deliberative systems.

In these contexts, preserving human responsibility may contribute directly to the purpose of the activity.

---

# Summary

Intentional Responsibility Allocation provides the operational mechanism through which Computational Facilitation can be applied.

Rather than framing automation as a binary choice, the framework encourages designers to examine each responsibility independently, allocating it according to the purpose of the activity and the complementary strengths of humans and computational systems.

This responsibility-centered approach enables Computational Facilitation to function as a practical design methodology applicable across a broad range of human-centered computational systems.

---

# Evaluation Framework

Computational Facilitation proposes that computational systems should be evaluated not only by their technical performance, but also by the quality of the human activities they enable.

Traditional software evaluation often emphasizes computational metrics such as correctness, efficiency, reliability, scalability, and latency.

While these metrics remain essential, they provide only a partial assessment for systems whose primary purpose is to facilitate human interaction.

The framework therefore proposes a complementary evaluation model consisting of two equally important dimensions:

- Computational Performance
- Human Outcomes

Both dimensions should be considered when assessing systems designed according to Computational Facilitation.

---

# Evaluation Dimension I — Purpose Alignment

The first evaluation criterion examines whether the allocation of responsibilities supports the fundamental purpose of the system.

Evaluation Questions:

- What is the primary purpose of the activity?
- Does automation strengthen that purpose?
- Does automation unintentionally replace the purpose?
- Are participants still actively engaged in the activity?

Examples:

A discussion platform exists to encourage discussion.

A multiplayer educational game exists to encourage collaborative learning.

A peer-review platform exists to facilitate scholarly evaluation.

Automation should therefore support these purposes rather than substitute for them.

---

# Evaluation Dimension II — Responsibility Allocation

The second criterion evaluates whether responsibilities have been intentionally assigned to the most appropriate participant.

Researchers should examine each significant responsibility individually.

Questions include:

- Which responsibilities are computational?
- Which responsibilities remain human?
- Is the allocation explicitly justified?
- Does the allocation preserve meaningful human participation?

Evaluation should focus on intentional design decisions rather than simply measuring the quantity of automation.

---

# Evaluation Dimension III — Human Outcomes

Because Computational Facilitation seeks to strengthen Human ↔ Human interaction, human outcomes constitute a primary measure of success.

Potential evaluation criteria include:

- quality of communication;
- collaboration;
- participant engagement;
- creativity;
- learning;
- negotiation;
- shared understanding;
- participant satisfaction.

The selection of appropriate outcome measures depends upon the purpose of the system under investigation.

---

# Evaluation Dimension IV — Computational Outcomes

Computational systems must continue to perform their infrastructural responsibilities reliably.

Relevant measures include:

- correctness;
- synchronization;
- fault tolerance;
- responsiveness;
- scalability;
- security;
- consistency;
- availability.

The framework therefore recognizes that preserving human responsibility should not come at the expense of computational reliability.

---

# Evaluation Dimension V — Balance

The final criterion considers the overall balance between computational support and human participation.

The objective is not to maximize either automation or manual effort.

Instead, researchers should evaluate whether the allocation of responsibilities remains consistent with the purpose of the activity.

Indicators of excessive computational dominance may include:

- reduced human engagement;
- diminished creativity;
- unnecessary replacement of human judgment;
- reduced opportunities for collaboration.

Indicators of insufficient computational support may include:

- repetitive manual work;
- unnecessary cognitive burden;
- poor coordination;
- avoidable errors;
- inefficient interaction.

An effective Computational Facilitation system seeks an appropriate balance rather than maximizing one participant's responsibilities at the expense of the other.

---

# Conceptual Evaluation Matrix

The framework may be operationalized using a qualitative evaluation matrix.

| Dimension | Evaluation Question |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Purpose Alignment | Does automation support the intended human activity? |
| Responsibility Allocation | Are responsibilities intentionally distributed? |
| Human Outcomes | Does the system strengthen meaningful human participation? |
| Computational Outcomes | Does computation reliably perform its assigned responsibilities? |
| Overall Balance | Does the final allocation preserve the purpose of the activity? |

The matrix is intended as a conceptual guide rather than a rigid scoring instrument.

Individual application domains may require additional criteria appropriate to their specific objectives.

---

# Applying the Evaluation Framework

Researchers applying Computational Facilitation should proceed through the following stages.

1. Define the primary purpose of the system.

2. Identify all major responsibilities.

3. Document the allocation of each responsibility.

4. Evaluate computational performance.

5. Evaluate human outcomes.

6. Examine whether the observed allocation remains aligned with the original purpose of the activity.

This process provides a structured method for evaluating systems that extend beyond traditional measures of computational performance.

---

# Summary

The Computational Facilitation Evaluation Framework expands conventional software evaluation by recognizing that systems designed to facilitate human activities should be judged not only by how effectively they compute, but also by how effectively they preserve, strengthen, and enrich meaningful human participation.

Successful systems therefore achieve excellence across both computational performance and human outcomes rather than optimizing one at the expense of the other.

---

# Boundary Conditions

Computational Facilitation is not proposed as a universal design framework for all computational systems.

Its applicability depends fundamentally on the purpose of the activity being supported.

The framework is intended primarily for systems in which meaningful human participation constitutes an essential component of the system's value.

Accordingly, Computational Facilitation should be understood as a domain-specific design framework rather than a general theory of automation.

---

# Appropriate Application Domains

The framework is expected to be most applicable where human interpretation, communication, creativity, collaboration, or judgment forms the primary purpose of the activity.

Illustrative examples include:

- educational platforms;
- multiplayer games;
- collaborative writing environments;
- peer-review systems;
- brainstorming tools;
- deliberation platforms;
- scientific collaboration;
- knowledge-sharing communities.

In these contexts, replacing human participation with computational automation may reduce the very qualities the system seeks to promote.

---

# Domains Where Extensive Automation Is Appropriate

The framework does not argue against automation in domains where automation directly supports the primary objective.

Examples include:

- numerical simulation;
- industrial process control;
- network routing;
- database management;
- image reconstruction;
- cryptographic computation;
- autonomous navigation under defined operational constraints.

In these systems, computational accuracy, consistency, efficiency, or safety frequently constitute the principal objectives.

Computational Facilitation therefore recognizes that extensive automation may be entirely appropriate.

---

# Mixed Domains

Many computational systems combine both infrastructural and interpretive responsibilities.

Examples include:

- clinical decision support;
- computer-aided design;
- educational tutoring systems;
- software development environments;
- scientific discovery platforms.

In such systems, Intentional Responsibility Allocation may be applied selectively to determine which responsibilities should remain human and which may be delegated to computational systems.

Rather than prescribing fixed allocations, the framework encourages explicit justification for each allocation decision.

---

# Limitations

As an emerging theoretical framework, Computational Facilitation has several limitations.

---

## Limited Empirical Validation

The framework is currently supported primarily by conceptual analysis and the initial implementation represented by The Last Word War™.

Additional empirical studies across diverse application domains are required before broader conclusions can be drawn.

---

## Context Dependence

Appropriate responsibility allocation is likely to depend upon the goals, participants, and context of a particular system.

Consequently, the framework does not prescribe universal allocations.

Instead, it proposes a process for making allocation decisions intentionally.

---

## Difficulty of Measuring Human Outcomes

Several important outcomes emphasized by the framework—including creativity, collaboration, and meaning-making—are inherently difficult to quantify.

Evaluation may therefore require mixed-method approaches combining quantitative and qualitative evidence.

---

## Evolving Computational Capabilities

As computational systems continue to evolve, responsibilities currently regarded as uniquely human may become increasingly automatable.

The framework therefore emphasizes preserving the purpose of an activity rather than preserving particular technical capabilities.

This distinction allows Computational Facilitation to remain relevant despite advances in computational technology.

---

# Potential Criticisms

The framework anticipates several possible criticisms.

---

## Criticism 1

### "The framework simply restates Human-Centered Computing."

Response:

Computational Facilitation does not seek to replace Human-Centered Computing.

Instead, it proposes a more specific framework for intentional responsibility allocation within systems whose primary purpose is meaningful Human ↔ Human interaction.

---

## Criticism 2

### "The boundary between human and computational responsibilities is subjective."

Response:

This criticism is well founded.

Responsibility allocation is expected to vary across domains and contexts.

The framework therefore emphasizes explicit reasoning rather than fixed prescriptions.

---

## Criticism 3

### "Increasing automation may improve human outcomes."

Response:

The framework fully acknowledges this possibility.

Computational Facilitation does not oppose automation.

Rather, it proposes evaluating automation according to whether it supports or undermines the purpose of the activity.

Automation remains appropriate whenever it strengthens that purpose.

---

## Criticism 4

### "Human judgment is not always superior."

Response:

The framework does not assume human superiority.

Instead, it recognizes complementary strengths.

Many computational responsibilities are performed more effectively by computational systems.

The framework concerns appropriate allocation rather than superiority.

---

## Criticism 5

### "The framework may be difficult to operationalize."

Response:

This challenge motivates the development of Intentional Responsibility Allocation and the Computational Facilitation Evaluation Framework.

Future empirical work should continue refining practical methods for applying and evaluating the framework.

---

# Falsifiability

A useful scientific framework should admit the possibility of being challenged by evidence.

Computational Facilitation may therefore be questioned or refined if future research demonstrates that:

- alternative responsibility allocations consistently produce superior human outcomes;
- preserving human responsibility does not contribute to the intended purpose of an activity;
- responsibility allocation proves unrelated to participant engagement, collaboration, or creativity;
- the framework cannot be applied consistently across comparable domains.

Such findings would not necessarily invalidate the framework entirely, but they would inform its refinement, scope, and applicability.

---

# Summary

Computational Facilitation is proposed as an evolving design framework rather than a completed theory.

Its value ultimately depends upon its ability to explain, guide, and improve the design of computational systems across multiple domains.

Future empirical studies, comparative analyses, and independent applications will determine the extent to which the framework generalizes beyond its initial case study in **The Last Word War™**.

# Research Questions and Future Research Agenda

Computational Facilitation is proposed as an evolving research framework rather than a completed theory.

Its long-term value depends upon empirical investigation across multiple computational domains.

Accordingly, the framework gives rise to several research questions intended to guide future theoretical, engineering, and experimental work.

These questions should be regarded as an ongoing research agenda rather than a fixed programme.

---

# Research Theme I — Responsibility Allocation

The first research theme concerns the allocation of responsibilities between computational systems and humans.

Representative questions include:

- How should responsibilities be classified?
- Which responsibilities consistently benefit from computational automation?
- Which responsibilities are most effectively preserved as human activities?
- How do different responsibility allocations influence system outcomes?

Future work may investigate whether consistent allocation patterns emerge across different application domains.

---

# Research Theme II — Human Outcomes

Computational Facilitation proposes that computational systems should be evaluated partly through their effects on meaningful human participation.

Important research questions include:

- Does preserving human responsibility increase engagement?
- Does responsibility allocation improve collaboration?
- Does preserving interpretation encourage creativity?
- How does responsibility allocation influence learning?
- What human outcomes are most appropriate for different application domains?

These questions require empirical investigation using both quantitative and qualitative methodologies.

---

# Research Theme III — Design Methodology

Intentional Responsibility Allocation (IRA) provides an operational methodology for Computational Facilitation.

Future work should investigate:

- whether designers can apply IRA consistently;
- whether different designers produce similar allocations;
- how responsibility allocation decisions evolve throughout software development;
- whether structured allocation improves system quality.

Comparative design studies may help evaluate the practical usefulness of IRA.

---

# Research Theme IV — Evaluation

The Computational Facilitation Evaluation Framework (CFEF) introduces a structured approach for evaluating human-centered computational systems.

Future studies should examine:

- the reliability of the proposed evaluation dimensions;
- relationships between computational performance and human outcomes;
- reproducibility across independent evaluators;
- opportunities for quantitative assessment.

Further refinement may ultimately produce standardized evaluation instruments for Computational Facilitation.

---

# Research Theme V — Domain Generalization

Although The Last Word War™ serves as the initial implementation of Computational Facilitation, the framework is intended to extend beyond multiplayer games.

Future research should explore applications in domains such as:

- education;
- collaborative writing;
- scientific peer review;
- online deliberation;
- citizen science;
- software engineering;
- creative collaboration.

Comparative studies across multiple domains will help determine the generality of the framework.

# Closing Remarks

Computational Facilitation is proposed not as a completed doctrine, but as a developing framework for designing computational systems that intentionally preserve meaningful human participation.

Its continued development depends upon open critique, empirical validation, interdisciplinary collaboration, and practical implementation across diverse application domains.

The framework should therefore be viewed as an invitation to further investigation rather than a final answer.

Its ultimate value will be determined not by philosophical appeal alone, but by its ability to guide the design of computational systems that demonstrably strengthen human interaction while preserving the uniquely human responsibilities that give those interactions their meaning.
